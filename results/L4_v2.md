**Summary:**
- int4 is now faster than fp16 on every layer.
- On the 7B layers it is 2.1–2.5× faster, where v1 reached 1.5–2.1×.
- The large layers sit at 47–57% of copy bandwidth. fp16 cuBLAS reaches 74–100% there.

## What changed in the explanation

v1 said bandwidth tracks the *number of programs*. v2 shows that this is only a proxy.

7B q/o_proj launch 448 programs both without split-K and with it, yet split-K is 1.57× faster:

| | BLOCK_N | Words per load | Bytes per load | K steps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No split-K | 8 | 8 × 16 | 512 B | 28 |
| Split-K | 32 | 32 × 16 | 2 KB | 7 |

The quantity that matters is **bytes in flight**: how much data the GPU has requested and not yet received at any moment. More programs raise it, and so do wider tiles. Split-K lets the kernel use wide tiles without running out of programs. For a memory-bound kernel this is the standard explanation (Little's law): bandwidth ≈ bytes in flight ÷ memory latency. Q5 was phrased in terms of program count, and it failed for that reason.

## What still limits the kernel

- **Large layers stop at about 50–57% of copy bandwidth.** That is a 2.1–2.5× speedup, against the 3.8× byte saving.
- **Small layers (< 20 µs) are bound by launch time and latency.** Split-K also adds a second kernel launch for the reduction. In a real decode loop, CUDA graphs remove most launch cost, so these numbers are pessimistic for small layers.

The remaining gap on the large layers is not explained yet. Candidates:
- The inner loop is too heavy per byte: unpacking, the int-to-float conversion and the 3-D reduction run on every 4-bit code.
- Loads are not vectorized: 32-bit loads where 128-bit loads would be possible.
- Too few bytes are in flight even now.

Nsight Compute on the 7B gate_up kernel would tell these apart: memory-throughput %, issue-slot utilisation, and the instruction mix. A useful reference point is vLLM's Marlin int4 kernel, which is reported to get close to the bandwidth roofline at batch 1.
